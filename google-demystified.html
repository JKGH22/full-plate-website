Get Found

Google, Demystified: What You Can Do Yourself to Get Found

November 2026 · 7 min read · Full Plate Marketing Co.

Most owners treat Google like the weather. Some weeks the phone rings, some weeks it goes quiet, and nobody can say why.

Google is more knowable than that. It publishes the basic rules for local search, and most of the work it rewards is free. We spent the last few weeks digging into how local rankings really move while building out our own search packages. Here is what we found, and what you can start on this week.

How Google picks the three businesses on the map

When someone searches for what you do, Google shows a map with three businesses under it. Google says those results come down to three things:

  • Relevance. How well your profile matches what the person searched.
  • Distance. How far you are from the person searching.
  • Prominence. How well known you are, online and off. Reviews, links and articles about your business all count here.

Your building stays where it is, so distance is mostly out of your hands. Relevance and prominence are yours to work on, and everything below feeds one of those two.

Good to know: Google states there is no way to pay for a better spot on the map. Ads are bought separately and labeled as sponsored. A good map ranking is earned.

Six things we found along the way

  1. Your ranking changes block by block. Because distance is measured from the person searching, you can be the top result from your front door and missing two miles down the road. Checking your ranking from one spot tells you very little. We check from a grid of points across a whole service area to see the real picture.
  2. Google retired the Q&A section. Starting in December 2025, Google began replacing the questions and answers on Business Profiles with an AI "Ask" feature in Maps. It builds answers from your profile, your reviews and your website. The details you write in those places now answer customer questions for you.
  3. Reviews work best as a steady stream. A burst of reviews in one week followed by months of quiet does less than a few every month. Ask every happy customer, and reply to every review, good or bad.
  4. Keywords in your business name can get your profile suspended. "Smith Roofing | Best Roofer Kill Devil Hills" breaks Google's guidelines. Use your real business name and list your services in the services section.
  5. Matching details matter more than people think. Your name, address and phone number should read exactly the same everywhere: your website, Facebook, Yelp, Apple Maps, Bing and industry directories. Changing your phone number or address means updating every one of them.
  6. Your website still does a lot of the work. Google reads it to understand what you do and where. A page for each main service, with your town in the page title, gives Google something specific to match.

The free checklist

Every item here costs nothing but time. Work through it in order.

Your Google Business Profile

  • Claim and verify your profile at business.google.com.
  • Choose the most specific primary category you can, then add secondary categories for your other services.
  • Fill in every field: hours (holiday hours too), service area, services, products, attributes and a plain description.
  • Add real photos regularly: outside, inside, your team and your work.
  • Share an update or offer every week or two.
  • Ask for reviews and reply to every one.

Your website

  • Put your name, address and phone number on every page, matching your profile.
  • Give each main service its own page, with the service and your town in the title.
  • Sign up for Google Search Console, which is free, to see which searches show your site.
  • Open your site on your phone. Most local searches happen there.

Around the web

  • Claim Apple Business Connect, Bing Places, Yelp and Facebook, and match your details on each.
  • Join your chamber of commerce and the directories for your industry. Links from trusted local sites build prominence.

AI search

  • Ask ChatGPT and Google who to call for what you do in your town. See whether you are named, and read what it says about you.

So why do people pay someone?

You can do most of this yourself. The checklist above is free, and a weekend gets you a long way.

The catch is that local search keeps moving. Profiles need fresh posts, reviews need replies, listings drift out of date, and rankings need checking from more than one spot. Links and features on other websites take relationships and time to earn. Most owners would rather spend those hours running their business, and they want it done right the first time.

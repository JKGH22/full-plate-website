Marketing Fundamentals

3 marketing laws every local business should know, from a book written in 1993.

Full Plate Marketing Co. October 14, 2026 8 min read

The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing by Al Ries and Jack Trout is older than most of the apps on your phone. It still explains why some local businesses become the name everyone says first, and why others work twice as hard to stay in the conversation. Here are the three laws that stuck with us, translated for owners on Main Street.

Quick summary

Marketing is a fight for a spot in your customer's memory. These three laws explain how that spot gets won and lost. Below: what each law says, the real stories behind it, what it looks like for a local business, and a quick exercise for each one.

Key takeaways

  • Law of the Category: if you cannot be first in your category, carve out a narrower one you can be first in
  • Law of the Ladder: customers rank businesses in their head, and your strategy depends on which rung you hold
  • Law of Line Extension: every new offer under your name changes what your name means
  • Most customers remember about seven brands in a busy category and only two or three in a quiet one
  • All three laws point to the same habit: decide the one thing you want to be known for, and protect it

Why a 30-year-old book still matters

Ries and Trout published The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing through HarperBusiness in 1993. The examples are full of beer brands, rental cars, and soft drinks. The technology is ancient. The ideas are about how human memory works, and human memory has changed a lot less than the internet has.

The core argument of the whole book fits in one line: marketing is a battle of perceptions, decided in the customer's mind. The business with the better product wins less often than the business people think of first.

For a local business, that battle is small and personal. A neighbor asks for a recommendation. Somebody searches on their phone. A visitor asks the front desk where to eat. In each of those moments, a few names come to mind. These three laws are about making sure yours is one of them.

Law #2: The Law of the Category

The idea: if you cannot be first in a category, set up a new category where you can be first.

The book opens this chapter with aviation. Charles Lindbergh made the first solo nonstop flight across the Atlantic in 1927, and everyone remembers him. Five years later, in 1932, Amelia Earhart made the same crossing and became one of the most famous pilots in history. She owned a brand new category: the first woman to fly solo across the Atlantic.

Earhart is remembered because she was first at something specific. Ries and Trout point to business examples too. Dell entered a crowded personal computer market as the first company to sell computers by phone. Charles Schwab opened the first discount brokerage instead of trying to be a slightly better full-price one.

A fun fact check. The book calls Earhart the third person to cross, after Lindbergh and Bert Hinkler. The Smithsonian lists her as the second person to fly solo nonstop across the Atlantic. Hinkler's famous 1931 crossing was over the South Atlantic. Either way, the lesson holds. History remembers her for the category she created: the first woman.

What it looks like for a local business

In a small town, "best restaurant" or "best plumber" is a category somebody already owns. Fighting for it head on is expensive. Narrow it until you can be first:

  • By customerThe first dog-friendly brunch spot in town. The first gym built for people over 50. The first realtor who only sells to second-home buyers.
  • By needThe first plumber with true same-day emergency service. The first salon that specializes in curly hair.
  • By deliveryThe first coffee shop with a drive-thru. The first contractor who sends a video estimate before the visit.

The key move is the question. Stop asking "how are we better?" and start asking "first at what?" Better is hard to prove. First is easy to remember.

Law #7: The Law of the Ladder

The idea: customers keep a mental ladder for every category, and the right strategy depends on which rung you occupy.

Picture the rental car ladder in the 1960s. Hertz on top, Avis second, National third. For years Avis tried to talk like a leader, running ads about being the finest in rent-a-cars. Customers knew Avis was second, so the message bounced off.

Then in 1962 the agency Doyle Dane Bernbach flipped it. Avis admitted it was only No. 2, and promised to try harder because of it. Customers believed the message because it matched what was already in their heads.

The same chapter explains how many rungs a ladder can hold. High-interest categories like cars or cereal hold up to about seven brands. Low-interest categories like car batteries or lawn mowers might hold two or three. Each rung tends to do roughly double the business of the rung below it.

Another example from the book: Royal Doulton, the second-place fine china brand, took on leader Lenox by pointing out that Royal Doulton was made in England. The leader was made in New Jersey. Second place, used honestly, became a selling point.

What it looks like for a local business

Local ladders are short. Ask ten people in your town to name an HVAC company and you will probably hear the same two or three names. That short list is your real competition, and your rung on it shapes your message:

  • If you are No. 1Your job is to stay top of mind and make the category bigger. Talk about why people need the service. Most of the new demand comes to you anyway.
  • If you are No. 2Be honest about it and give people a reason to pick you. Faster response, a more personal touch, a specific specialty. The Avis move works because it is believable.
  • If you are further downGo back to Law #2. Find a narrower category where you can sit on the top rung of a shorter ladder.

The biggest mistake on this law is claiming a rung customers know belongs to someone else. "The best in town" from a business nobody has heard of reads as noise. A specific, honest claim reads as confidence.

Law #12: The Law of Line Extension

The idea: there is constant pressure to stretch a successful name across new products, and stretching it usually weakens it.

7-Up is the poster child. The brand added Diet 7-Up, Cherry 7-Up, Diet Cherry 7-Up, 7-Up Gold, and more. Each launch made sense in a meeting. Together they blurred what 7-Up meant, and the brand's market share fell by more than half, from 5.7% to 2.5%.

Miller tells the same story at a bigger scale. Around Miller High Life came Miller Lite, Miller Genuine Draft, Miller Reserve, Miller Clear, and more. In a 2003 essay, Al Ries pointed out that Miller High Life fell from 23.6 million barrels in 1979 to 5.3 million in 2002. His diagnosis was line extension.

What it looks like for a local business

This is the law local owners break most, usually for good reasons. A slow season pushes you to add a service. A customer asks if you also do X, so you start doing X. A neighbor's idea sounds like easy money. Five years later the business does a dozen things, and nobody can say what it is best at.

Some examples of how it creeps in:

  • The coffee shopAdds smoothies, then sandwiches, then catering, then merch. The espresso that made it famous gets less attention every year.
  • The landscaperAdds pressure washing, holiday lights, and gutter cleaning. Each one pays, and the name drifts from "the yard people" to "a guy who does stuff."
  • The marketing agencyYes, us too. Every new service we add has to answer one question: does it make our main promise stronger, or does it pull our name somewhere new?

Adding to the menu is fine. The test is whether the new thing strengthens the reason people already come to you. If it needs a different reputation to sell, it may deserve a different name.

Where the book shows its age. Some line extensions have clearly worked. Bud Light eventually outsold Budweiser itself. Ries and Trout would answer that the light beer category was so big it became its own ladder, and that the original brand paid for it. Treat the laws as strong defaults, and question them when you have a clear reason.

How the three laws fit together

Read side by side, the laws describe one cycle. You win a spot by being first at something specific. You hold it by being honest about where you stand. You lose it by stretching your name until it stands for everything.

The thread running through all three is focus is a marketing decision. Picking what you will be known for matters as much as any ad you run.

The 10-minute worksheet. Copy it, fill it in.

1. Category: We are the first (or only) ______ for ______ in ______.

2. Ladder: When people around here think of my category, the first three names they say are ______, ______, ______. I am on rung ___. My honest reason to choose us is ______.

3. Line Extension: The one thing I want my name to mean is ______. Every service I offer that does not strengthen that is: ______.

Frequently asked questions

Is The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing still worth reading?

Yes. It was published in 1993, so the brand examples are dated, but the core ideas about how customers remember businesses hold up well. It is short enough to read in an afternoon.

Do these laws apply to small local businesses or just big brands?

They apply to anyone competing for a spot in a customer's memory. In a small town the ladder is shorter, so knowing where you stand matters even more.

How do I find a category I can be first in?

Narrow by customer, need, or delivery. Ask what you do that nobody nearby claims, then say it plainly and repeat it everywhere.

Is it ever smart to add a new product or service?

Yes, when it strengthens the one thing you want to be known for. The risk comes from offers that pull your name in a new direction. If it needs a different reputation to sell, consider a separate name.

How do I know where my business sits on the ladder?

Ask customers and neighbors to name the first three businesses that come to mind in your category. Their answers are your ladder, whatever your sales numbers say.
